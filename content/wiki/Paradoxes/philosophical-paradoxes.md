# Philosophical Paradoxes

<p class="at-lead">The most basic concepts we rely on — identity, knowledge, goodness, freedom — collapse into contradiction when examined with sufficient rigor. Philosophical paradoxes do not merely puzzle; they map the fault lines where ordinary language and intuitive reasoning give way under pressure.</p>

## Paradox cards

> [!grid]
>
>> [!card] **Ship of Theseus**
>> **Setup:** A ship has every plank replaced over time; the discarded originals are reassembled into a second vessel.
>>
>> **Bite:** Which is the original? Identity cannot rest solely on parts or solely on function without absurd consequences.
>>
>> **Resolution:** [[mystical-paradoxes|Non-self metaphysics]] and four-dimensionalism treat objects as temporal worms; functionalism and mereological nihilism offer rival solutions. None commands consensus.
>
>> [!card] **Sorites Paradox**
>> **Setup:** One grain of sand is not a heap, and adding a single grain never creates a heap — yet a million grains clearly do.
>>
>> **Bite:** Vague predicates seem to lack sharp boundaries, but classical logic demands that every statement be true or false.
>>
>> **Resolution:** Supervaluationism and degree theories each preserve logical structure while accommodating vagueness.
>
>> [!card] **Paradox of Analysis**
>> **Setup:** A conceptual analysis must be both correct and informative.
>>
>> **Bite:** If the analysans is synonymous with the analysandum, it is uninformative; if not, it is incorrect.
>>
>> **Resolution:** Distinguish understanding from knowing-that, or reject the strict synonymy requirement.
>
>> [!card] **Buridan's Bridge**
>> **Setup:** Plato promises to let Socrates cross if his next statement is true, else throw him in the water. Socrates says, "You will throw me in the water."
>>
>> **Bite:** Whatever Plato does, he seems to break his promise.
>>
>> **Resolution:** The paradox exploits self-reference and ambiguous metalanguage; modern solutions use stratified truth predicates.
>
>> [!card] **Paradox of Fiction**
>> **Setup:** People experience strong emotions for characters they know do not exist.
>>
>> **Bite:** Genuine emotion seems to require belief in the object’s existence.
>>
>> **Resolution:** Pretense theory, thought theory, and illusion theory explain how quasi-emotions arise from imagination.
>
>> [!card] **Fitch's Paradox**
>> **Setup:** If all truths are knowable, then all truths must in fact be known.
>>
>> **Bite:** An apparently modest claim about the limits of knowledge collapses into omniscience.
>>
>> **Resolution:** Restrict the knowability thesis to non-indexical truths, or reject the modal logic driving the proof.
>
>> [!card] **Paradox of Free Will**
>> **Setup:** God knows in advance every decision a person will make.
>>
>> **Bite:** If the future is known, it seems fixed; if fixed, choice is illusory.
>>
>> **Resolution:** Compatibilism distinguishes foreknowledge from causal determination; [[philosophical-paradoxes|free-will]] defenses invoke libertarian agency.
>
>> [!card] **Goodman's Paradox**
>> **Setup:** All observed emeralds are green, but "grue" (green before time *t*, blue after) is equally supported by the same evidence.
>>
>> **Bite:** Induction appears unable to distinguish lawlike from accidentally true generalizations.
>>
>> **Resolution:** Goodman’s entrenchment thesis privileges predicates with longer histories of successful projection.
>
>> [!card] **Paradox of Hedonism**
>> **Setup:** Direct pursuit of happiness yields misery; indirect pursuit through other ends yields happiness.
>>
>> **Bite:** The target of a desire vanishes when it becomes the sole object of desire.
>>
>> **Resolution:** Happiness is a by-product of instrumental value, not an achievable end in itself.
>
>> [!card] **Liberal Paradox**
>> **Setup:** "Minimal Liberty" is incompatible with Pareto optimality.
>>
>> **Bite:** Even trivial personal freedom can block unanimous social improvements.
>>
>> **Resolution:** Domain restrictions or treating rights as side-constraints rather than preference orderings.
>
>> [!card] **Meno's Paradox**
>> **Setup:** One cannot seek what one knows, for one already has it; nor what one does not know, for one cannot recognize it.
>>
>> **Bite:** Learning appears impossible.
>>
>> **Resolution:** Plato's theory of recollection; modern epistemology rejects the exhaustive dichotomy.
>
>> [!card] **Mere Addition Paradox**
>> **Setup:** Is a vast population living barely tolerable lives better than a small, happy population?
>>
>> **Bite:** Total and average utilitarianism diverge sharply.
>>
>> **Resolution:** Parfit's repugnant conclusion challenges pure totalist views; pluralist axiology is one response.
>
>> [!card] **Moore's Paradox**
>> **Setup:** "It is raining, but I do not believe that it is."
>>
>> **Bite:** The assertion is absurd even though both conjuncts could be true.
>>
>> **Resolution:** Asserting a proposition while denying belief in it undermines the speech act; belief is partly constitutive of assertion.
>
>> [!card] **Newcomb's Paradox**
>> **Setup:** A predictor fills boxes based on a prediction of your choice; dominance clashes with expected utility.
>>
>> **Bite:** Two-boxers gain $1,000, one-boxers gain $1,000,000 — yet two-boxing seems always better.
>>
>> **Resolution:** Treated fully in [[decision-theory-paradoxes]].
>
>> [!card] **Paradox of Nihilism**
>> **Setup:** Several distinct paradoxes share the name, including the claim that nihilism is self-defeating.
>>
>> **Bite:** If nothing matters, then the truth of nihilism does not matter either.
>>
>> **Resolution:** Distinguish existential, moral, and epistemological nihilism; each has different implications.
>
>> [!card] **Omnipotence Paradox**
>> **Setup:** Can an omnipotent being create a rock so heavy that even it cannot lift it?
>>
>> **Bite:** Either answer seems to limit omnipotence.
>>
>> **Resolution:** Logical-limits response: omnipotence is the ability to do anything logically possible, not to perform contradictions.
>
>> [!card] **Polanyi's Paradox**
>> **Setup:** We know more than we can tell.
>>
>> **Bite:** Tacit knowledge resists explicit articulation, yet guides action reliably.
>>
>> **Resolution:** Embodied cognition and implicit learning; not all knowledge is propositional.
>
>> [!card] **Preface Paradox**
>> **Setup:** An author believes every statement in the book is correct, yet also believes at least one is incorrect.
>>
>> **Bite:** Rational belief in each conjunct seems compatible with rational belief in their collective negation.
>>
>> **Resolution:** Distinguish individual and collective justification; fallibilism permits both attitudes.
>
>> [!card] **Problem of Evil**
>> **Setup:** An omnipotent, omniscient, morally perfect God would prevent evil; evil exists.
>>
>> **Bite:** Theism seems rationally untenable.
>>
>> **Resolution:** Free-will defense, skeptical theism, and theodicy each attempt reconciliation.
>
>> [!card] **Rule-Following Paradox**
>> **Setup:** No course of action can be determined by a rule, because any action can be made to accord with it.
>>
>> **Bite:** Rules seem both necessary and impossible for guiding behavior.
>>
>> **Resolution:** Wittgenstein's appeal to communal practice and criteria; private language is rejected.
>
>> [!card] **When a White Horse Is Not a Horse**
>> **Setup:** White horses are not horses because "white" and "horse" refer to different things.
>>
>> **Bite:** A seemingly true statement about categories leads to absurd conclusions.
>>
>> **Resolution:** Distinguish intension and extension; the paradox exploits conflation of properties and classes.
>
>> [!card] **Zeno's Paradoxes**
>> **Setup:** Motion is impossible because one must first traverse half the distance, then half of what remains, ad infinitum.
>>
>> **Bite:** The infinite divisibility of space seems to freeze all change.
>>
>> **Resolution:** Treated fully in [[mathematical-paradoxes]].

## Deep dives

### Ship of Theseus

The Ship of Theseus asks whether an object that has had all of its components replaced remains fundamentally the same object. If a ship has every plank, nail, and sail replaced over centuries, is the restored vessel still the Ship of Theseus? What if the discarded original parts are reassembled into a second ship — which of the two is the genuine original?

The puzzle is not merely about ships. It strikes at the heart of **diachronic identity**: what makes an entity the same entity across time? Modern variants multiply the problem. Human bodies replace nearly every cell over roughly seven years; does this mean we are not the same persons we once were? In software engineering, **versioning** raises the same question: is a program the "same" application if every line of code has been rewritten? DNA provides a biological parallel: a living organism continuously metabolizes and rebuilds its molecular structure, yet we treat it as one persistent individual.

Philosophers have proposed several solutions. **Four-dimensionalism** treats objects as four-dimensional "worms" extended through time; the ship-at-each-moment is a temporal slice, and the whole worm is the object. **Functionalism** says identity is preserved as long as the same role or function continues. A more radical **mereological nihilist** denies that composite objects exist at all, leaving only fundamental particles. Each response carries costs. If function alone secures identity, then a perfect replica built from scratch would be indistinguishable from the original, which many find counter-intuitive.

The paradox echoes themes explored in [[mystical-paradoxes]]: Buddhist metaphysics of *anatta* (non-self) and Heraclitus's river both deny stable identity across change.

### Sorites Paradox (The Heap)

If one grain of sand does not make a heap, and adding a single grain to a non-heap never creates a heap, then no finite number of grains can form a heap. Yet a million grains clearly do. The argument is valid; one of its premises must fail.

The paradox exposes **vagueness**: terms like "heap," "bald," and "tall" lack sharp boundaries. Classical bivalent logic demands that every statement be true or false, but vague predicates resist such classification.

**Supervaluationism** offers a prominent response. It holds that a vague statement is true if it comes out true on every acceptable way of making the predicate precise (every "precisification" or "sharpening"). "One grain is not a heap" is true on all sharpenings; "A million grains is a heap" is true on all; but "Ten thousand grains is a heap" is neither true nor false because some sharpenings make it true and others false. This preserves classical logic while accommodating vagueness.

**Degree theories** take a different route, assigning truth-values between 0 and 1. "This is a heap" might be 0.73 true for ten thousand grains. This fits naturally with fuzzy logic and cognitive models of gradable adjectives, though it challenges the classical law of excluded middle.

### Omnipotence Paradox

Can an omnipotent being create a rock so heavy that even it cannot lift it? If yes, then there exists a task the being cannot perform (lifting the rock), so it is not omnipotent. If no, then there exists a task it cannot perform (creating such a rock), so again it is not omnipotent.

The standard **logical-limits response** distinguishes abilities from logical impossibilities. Omnipotence is defined as the ability to do anything that is logically possible. Creating a married bachelor or a rock too heavy for an omnipotent being to lift is not a task at all — it is a contradiction in terms, like drawing a square circle. An omnipotent being cannot perform nonsense, but this is no limitation on its power, just as the inability to destroy the law of non-contradiction is no weakness.

Alternative formulations replace the rock with tasks like "creating a being more powerful than itself" or "making 2 + 2 = 5." Defenders of the logical-limits view extend the same reply: these are not genuinely describable acts but malformed descriptions. Critics, however, worry that this makes omnipotence merely "the greatest power consistent with logic," which some theists find too thin.

### Problem of Evil

The Epicurean formulation is stark: "Is God willing to prevent evil, but not able? Then he is not omnipotent. Is he able, but not willing? Then he is malevolent. Is he both able and willing? Then whence cometh evil?"

This is the most powerful argument against theism in the Western canon. It does not merely note that suffering exists; it asserts that the coexistence of an all-powerful, all-knowing, all-good God with evil is **logically impossible**.

Responses cluster into three families:

**Free-will defense** (Plantinga): God values libertarian free will. A world with free creatures who sometimes choose evil may be better than a world of deterministic automatons who always choose good, because the former contains morally significant love, courage, and sacrifice. God cannot both create free beings and unilaterally prevent all evil without undermining freedom itself. The paradox of free will intersects closely with this defense; see [[philosophical-paradoxes|free-will]] for the broader tension between foreknowledge and agency.

**Skeptical theism**: Human cognitive limitations are vast. We cannot see the full tapestry of reasons God might have for permitting a given evil. The inference "I cannot see a reason, therefore no reason exists" is an argument from ignorance. Just as a novice chess player cannot fathom why a grandmaster sacrifices a queen, we may be unable to grasp why God permits suffering.

**Theodicy** (Leibniz, Hick): Some theists offer positive reasons — soul-making, the greater good, the necessity of contrast — arguing that evil is instrumental to a higher-order good that outweighs it.

### Zeno's Paradoxes

Zeno of Elea argued that motion is impossible: to travel from A to B, one must first reach the halfway point; but to reach that, one must reach the quarter-way point, and so on ad infinitum. Since an infinite number of tasks cannot be completed, motion cannot begin.

Modern mathematics resolves the paradox through the theory of **convergent infinite series**: the infinite sum 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ... converges to 1 in finite time. Yet the paradox persists philosophically: does the mathematical model genuinely capture the physical process of traversal? These paradoxes are treated fully in [[mathematical-paradoxes]].

### Newcomb's Paradox

A superintelligent Predictor has placed $1,000 in Box A and either $1,000,000 or nothing in Box B, based on a prediction of your choice. If the Predictor predicted you would take only Box B, it put the million in; if it predicted you would take both, Box B is empty. You know all this. Do you take one box or two?

Dominance reasoning says take both — Box B's contents are fixed, so you gain $1,000 either way. Expected utility, however, says one-boxers end up rich and two-boxers end up poor, so rationality demands taking only Box B. The paradox reveals a clash between causal decision theory and evidential decision theory. It is treated fully in [[decision-theory-paradoxes]].

### Moore's Paradox

G. E. Moore observed the absurdity of asserting a proposition while denying belief in it: "It is raining, but I do not believe that it is." Neither conjunct is contradictory on its own; jointly they produce a pragmatic absurdity.

The explanation lies in the **norms of assertion**. To assert *p* is to present yourself as believing *p* and having evidence for *p*. Asserting *p* while simultaneously denying belief in *p* undermines the speech act itself. It is like promising to do something while announcing you have no intention of doing it — the promise is not false but hollow. Some philosophers extend this to argue that belief is partly constitutive of sincere assertion, making Moorean sentences not merely odd but genuinely inconsistent in a broad sense.

### Meno's Paradox

Plato's *Meno* poses the **learning paradox**: if you already know what you are searching for, inquiry is unnecessary; if you do not know, you cannot recognize it when found. Either way, learning seems impossible.

Plato's response is the **theory of recollection**: the soul is immortal and has already learned everything; what we call learning is really recollecting latent knowledge triggered by questioning. Socrates's midwifery in the *Meno* demonstrates this: by asking the right questions, he elicits geometric knowledge from an uneducated slave boy.

Modern epistemology dissolves the dilemma by rejecting its exhaustive dichotomy. We can have **partial knowledge** — knowing what kind of thing to look for without knowing the specific instance. Heuristic search, pattern recognition, and abductive inference all describe learning that is neither full possession nor total ignorance.

### Goodman's Paradox (Grue)

Nelson Goodman's "new riddle of induction" asks why the hypothesis "All emeralds are green" is lawlike and projectible, while "All emeralds are grue" (green before time *t*, blue thereafter) is not, even though both are equally supported by every emerald observed so far.

Goodman argued that projectibility depends on **entrenchment**: predicates that have been used in successful inductive inferences become entrenched. "Green" has a long history of reliable projection; "grue" is gerrymandered. The paradox reveals that induction is not merely a matter of counting instances but of privileging certain predicates over others. This challenges the logical empiricist hope that inductive logic could be purely syntactic.
