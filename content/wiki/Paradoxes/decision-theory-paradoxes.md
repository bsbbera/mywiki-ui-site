# Decision Theory Paradoxes

<p class="at-lead">Rational choice theory promises that if we rank outcomes, assign probabilities, and maximise expected utility, we will never choose badly. Yet when pushed to its limits, the theory prescribes actions that no human would follow — and in doing so, reveals the cracks in its own foundations.</p>

> [!grid]
>
>> [!card] **Abilene paradox**
>> **Setup:** A group collectively decides on a course of action that nobody individually wants.
>> **Bite:** Rational aggregation of preferences yields an outcome every member dislikes.
>> **Resolution:** False consensus and pluralistic ignorance drive the group toward a mutually assumed but non-existent shared preference.
>
>> [!card] **Apportionment paradox**
>> **Setup:** Rounding seat allocations in proportional representation can produce counter-intuitive results.
>> **Bite:** Increasing total seats can shrink a bloc's representation (Alabama paradox), or adding a new state can raise another's votes (new-states paradox).
>> **Resolution:** Integer constraints on discrete seats make perfect proportionality impossible; no divisor method satisfies all fairness axioms simultaneously.
>
>> [!card] **Arrow's paradox**
>> **Setup:** Arrow's impossibility theorem proves that no voting system can satisfy a minimal set of fairness criteria when there are more than two candidates.
>> **Bite:** Democracy seems impossible to design rationally.
>> **Resolution:** The theorem applies only to ranked-choice systems and universal domain; restricting preferences or allowing cardinal utilities restores possibility.
>
>> [!card] **Buridan's ass**
>> **Setup:** A perfectly rational donkey placed exactly midway between two identical bales of hay starves because it has no reason to prefer one over the other.
>> **Bite:** Indifference paralyses choice.
>> **Resolution:** Real agents break ties randomly or use secondary criteria; Buridan's ass is a limit case, not a practical problem.
>
>> [!card] **Chainstore paradox**
>> **Setup:** In repeated chain-store games, predatory pricing to deter entrants is irrational in the final round, so by backward induction it is irrational in every round.
>> **Bite:** Yet real firms do engage in predatory pricing.
>> **Resolution:** Bounded rationality, reputation effects, and incomplete information sustain behaviour that strict backward induction forbids.
>
>> [!card] **Decision-making paradox**
>> **Setup:** Selecting the best decision-making method is itself a decision problem.
>> **Bite:** Meta-decisions require meta-methods, leading to infinite regress.
>> **Resolution:** In practice we satisfice at the meta-level, using simple heuristics to choose methods.
>
>> [!card] **Ellsberg paradox**
>> **Setup:** People prefer bets with known probabilities over bets with ambiguous probabilities, even when the expected utilities are identical.
>> **Bite:** This contradicts expected utility theory, which cares only about outcomes and probabilities, not their source.
>> **Resolution:** Ambiguity aversion is a robust empirical finding; models like prospect theory and robust decision theory incorporate it by penalising imprecise probabilities.
>
>> [!card] **Fenno's paradox**
>> **Setup:** Americans disapprove of Congress as a whole but approve of their own representative.
>> **Bite:** The whole is less than the sum of its parts.
>> **Resolution:** Localised benefits, constituent service, and partisan sorting create individual approval even while institutional performance is criticised.
>
>> [!card] **Fredkin's paradox**
>> **Setup:** The more similar two choices are, the more time a rational agent should spend deciding — yet the benefit of correct choice shrinks.
>> **Bite:** We invest most effort where it matters least.
>> **Resolution:** Optimal stopping rules and satisficing avoid this trap by setting decision-effort thresholds.
>
>> [!card] **Green paradox**
>> **Setup:** Policies intended to reduce future CO₂ emissions may increase present emissions.
>> **Bite:** A carbon tax announced for 2030 can cause firms to extract and sell fossil fuels now before the tax bites.
>> **Resolution:** Policy design must account for inter-temporal leakage; immediate global coordination reduces the incentive to front-load emissions.
>
>> [!card] **Hedgehog's dilemma**
>> **Setup:** Despite goodwill, human intimacy cannot occur without mutual harm.
>> **Bite:** The closer we draw, the more we hurt each other.
>> **Resolution:** Optimal distance balances warmth and pain; the paradox is more literary than mathematical.
>
>> [!card] **Inventor's paradox**
>> **Setup:** It is often easier to solve a more general problem than the specific one at hand.
>> **Bite:** Abstraction reveals structure that detail obscures.
>> **Resolution:** Generalisation can expose symmetries and invariants that simplify the search space.
>
>> [!card] **Kavka's toxin puzzle**
>> **Setup:** You are offered a reward for *intending* to drink a non-lethal toxin tomorrow; you need not actually drink it.
>> **Bite:** If the intention is sincere, why not drink? If you plan to drink, can you sincerely intend to do so while knowing you will not need to?
>> **Resolution:** The puzzle challenges the idea that intentions are simply predictions; they are commitments that resist strategic manipulation.
>
>> [!card] **Motivation crowding theory**
>> **Setup:** Adding financial incentives for a behaviour can reduce the behaviour.
>> **Bite:** More motivation yields less action.
>> **Resolution:** Extrinsic incentives can displace intrinsic motivation when the agent interprets the payment as signalling that the task is unpleasant or controlling.
>
>> [!card] **Morton's fork**
>> **Setup:** A false dilemma in which contradictory observations lead to the same conclusion.
>> **Bite:** No matter what the evidence says, the arguer wins.
>> **Resolution:** Expose the excluded middle; the two prongs are not exhaustive.
>
>> [!card] **Navigation paradox**
>> **Setup:** Increased navigational precision may increase collision risk.
>> **Bite:** Better technology makes the system less safe.
>> **Resolution:** Higher precision leads to tighter traffic patterns and less margin for error; systemic risk rises even as individual error falls.
>
>> [!card] **Newcomb's paradox**
>> **Setup:** A super-intelligent Predictor has placed $1M in a transparent box if it predicted you will take only that box; Box B always contains $1,000.
>> **Bite:** Causal decision theory says take both ($1,001,000); evidential decision theory says take only Box A ($1,000,000). Which is rational?
>> **Resolution:** The paradox divides causal and evidential theorists; superintelligence and pre-commitment arguments favour one-boxing.
>
>> [!card] **No-show paradox**
>> **Setup:** In some voting systems, voting for your candidate can cause them to lose.
>> **Bite:** Participation hurts your own cause.
>> **Resolution:** Instant-runoff and similar systems can exhibit non-monotonicity; the paradox motivates research into monotonic voting rules.
>
>> [!card] **Paradox of voting**
>> **Setup:** For a rational, self-interested voter, the expected benefit of voting is usually less than the cost.
>> **Bite:** Why does anyone vote?
>> **Resolution:** Expressive benefits, social norms, and altruistic concern for others' outcomes supplement narrow self-interest.
>
>> [!card] **Parrondo's paradox**
>> **Setup:** Two losing games, played in alternation or in random sequence, can produce a winning outcome.
>> **Bite:** Two negatives make a positive.
>> **Resolution:** The games are not independent; switching breaks negative correlations and allows ratchet-like accumulation of wins.
>
>> [!card] **Preparedness paradox**
>> **Setup:** Preparing for a catastrophe and reducing its damage makes the catastrophe seem less serious in hindsight.
>> **Bite:** Success in prevention undermines the political will to fund prevention.
>> **Resolution:** The paradox is a cognitive bias; objective risk reduction should be measured independently from subjective impact.
>
>> [!card] **Prevention paradox**
>> **Setup:** For one person to benefit, many must change behaviour even though most receive no direct benefit.
>> **Bite:** Public health interventions impose costs on the many for the sake of the few.
>> **Resolution:** Population-level prevention targets risk distributions; the net benefit is positive even when most individuals are unaffected.
>
>> [!card] **Prisoner's dilemma**
>> **Setup:** Two prisoners must choose to cooperate or defect without communication. Defecting dominates regardless of the other's choice, yet mutual cooperation is better than mutual defection.
>> **Bite:** Rational self-interest leads to a worse outcome for everyone.
>> **Resolution:** Iterated play, reputation, and altruistic punishment allow cooperation to evolve; institutions can enforce binding agreements.
>
>> [!card] **Voting paradox**
>> **Setup:** A group of rational individuals can have collectively irrational preferences (Condorcet cycles).
>> **Bite:** Majority rule can cycle indefinitely with no stable winner.
>> **Resolution:** Restricting the domain of preferences (e.g., single-peakedness) or using cardinal utilities eliminates cycles.
>
>> [!card] **Willpower paradox**
>> **Setup:** Those who keep their minds open are more goal-directed than those who declare objectives to themselves.
>> **Bite:** Explicit commitment reduces motivation.
>> **Resolution:** Mental contrasting (balancing fantasy with obstacles) outperforms pure positive thinking; openness may reflect more adaptive planning.

## Deep dive: Prisoner's dilemma

The canonical payoff matrix:

|  | B cooperates | B defects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A cooperates** | 3, 3 | 0, 5 |
| **A defects** | 5, 0 | 1, 1 |

Defection dominates for each player individually, yet (Cooperate, Cooperate) Pareto-dominates (Defect, Defect). In the iterated version, Robert Axelrod's tournaments showed that **tit-for-tat** — cooperate first, then mirror the opponent's last move — performs robustly well. The dilemma underpins everything from nuclear deterrence to climate agreements, showing that [[game-theory]] rationality and social optima need not coincide.

## Deep dive: Newcomb's paradox

Newcomb's paradox pits two decision theories against each other:

- **Causal decision theory (CDT):** Your choice cannot *cause* the Predictor's past action, so take both boxes and get $1,001,000 if the million is there, or $1,000 if not.
- **Evidential decision theory (EDT):** Taking one box is strong evidence that the Predictor foresaw this and placed the million; taking both boxes is evidence it left Box A empty.

The paradox has spawned a cottage industry of solutions: superrationality, timeless decision theory, and even quantum approaches. It remains unresolved, serving as a boundary marker between competing conceptions of rational agency.

## Deep dive: St. Petersburg paradox

A casino offers a coin-flip game: win $2 if the first head appears on flip 1, $4 on flip 2, $8 on flip 3, and so on. The expected value is infinite:

$$E = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^n} \cdot 2^n = \infty$$

Yet no one will pay more than a modest fee to play. Daniel Bernoulli resolved this by introducing diminishing marginal utility — a logarithmic utility curve makes the game's certainty equivalent finite. Modern behavioural economists add probability weighting and loss aversion to explain why even the utility-corrected version feels overpriced.

## Deep dive: Allais paradox

Maurice Allais showed that people's choices violate the independence axiom of expected utility theory:

- Most prefer **A:** $1M guaranteed over **B:** 10 % chance of $5M, 89 % chance of $1M, 1 % chance of nothing.
- But most also prefer **D:** 10 % chance of $5M over **C:** 11 % chance of $1M.

The preference for A over B implies a specific risk aversion, yet the same risk aversion should make C preferable to D. The inconsistency reveals that people treat outcomes as relative to a reference point, not as final wealth states — a cornerstone of prospect theory.

## Deep dive: Ellsberg paradox

Consider two urns:

- **Urn I:** 50 red, 50 black balls.
- **Urn II:** 100 red-or-black balls in unknown proportion.

Most people prefer to bet on red from Urn I than from Urn II, and also prefer to bet on black from Urn I than from Urn II. This violates expected utility theory, which requires indifference if the subjective probability of red equals that of black. The preference for known risks over ambiguous ones — **ambiguity aversion** — has been confirmed across cultures and species.

## Interesting facts

- The Allais paradox was presented at a 1952 conference in Paris attended by leading economists and statisticians; many initially refused to believe the results.
- Robert Axelrod's iterated prisoner's dilemma tournaments were run on punch-card computers in the 1970s; tit-for-tat won without ever defeating any opponent in a single round.
- Newcomb's paradox is named after physicist William Newcomb, who formulated it while working at the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory.
