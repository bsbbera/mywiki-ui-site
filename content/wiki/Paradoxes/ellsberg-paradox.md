# Ellsberg Paradox

<p class="at-lead">
The Ellsberg Paradox demonstrates that people systematically prefer choices with known probabilities over choices with ambiguous probabilities — contradicting expected utility theory, which treats all uncertainty equivalently.
</p>

## Overview

Daniel Ellsberg (later famous for the Pentagon Papers) presented the paradox in 1961 using two urns: one with 50 red and 50 black balls (known probabilities), and one with an unknown mix of red and black balls. People prefer to bet on the known urn, even though no rational basis exists for preferring one colour over the other in either case.

This reveals **ambiguity aversion** — a distinction between risk (known probabilities) and uncertainty (unknown probabilities). The finding motivated robust decision theory, Choquet expected utility, and multiple-priors models. It also connects to Knightian uncertainty in economics and the distinction between risk and ambiguity in behavioural finance.
