# Edgar F. Codd

<p class="at-lead">
British mathematician and IBM researcher who published a single 1970 paper that fundamentally restructured computing — the relational model, which gave us tables, relational algebra, and ultimately SQL, forming the theoretical foundation that every relational database from Oracle to PostgreSQL is built upon, earning him the Turing Award in 1981.
</p>

<span class="at-kicker">Biography</span>

## Background

British computer scientist (19 August 1923 – 18 April 2003), born in **Fortuneswell on the Isle of Portland**, Dorset, England. Studied **mathematics at Oxford University** on a full scholarship. Served as a **Flight Lieutenant in the Royal Air Force** (1942–1946) during World War II. After a brief stint as a mathematics lecturer at the **University of Tennessee** (1949), he joined **IBM** and spent the bulk of his career there (1949–1984, with a brief gap 1953–1957 at Computing Devices of Canada).

In **1970**, published the landmark paper *"A Relational Model of Data for Large Shared Data Banks"* in *Communications of the ACM* — a paper that fundamentally changed computing. It introduced the **relational model**: data stored in tables (relations) with columns (attributes) and rows (tuples), manipulated via **relational algebra**. This theoretical foundation became the basis for every SQL database — Oracle, MySQL, PostgreSQL, SQL Server, DB2 all exist because of this paper.

Subsequently defined **Codd's 12 rules** (later extended to 13) — a benchmark for determining whether a database truly qualifies as a relational DBMS — and co-developed **Boyce-Codd Normal Form (BCNF)** with Raymond Boyce. Won the **1981 ACM Turing Award** for *"his fundamental and continuing contributions to the theory and practice of database management systems."* Forbes (2002) listed Codd's relational model as one of the **most important innovations of the previous 85 years**.

<span class="at-kicker">Key Contributions</span>

## Key contributions

> [!grid|cols3]
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ### Relational Model (1970)
>> Tables, relations, and relational algebra — the foundation of SQL. Published in *Communications of the ACM*, this paper introduced the concept of storing data in tables manipulated by set operations, displacing the then-dominant hierarchical and network database models.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ### Codd's 12 Rules
>> The benchmark for a true RDBMS — a set of 12 (later 13) rules defining what it means for a database to be genuinely relational. These rules shaped the design of every commercial RDBMS and continue to serve as evaluation criteria.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ### Boyce-Codd Normal Form (BCNF)
>> Refinement of 3NF for **database normalization**, co-developed with Raymond Boyce. BCNF closes loopholes in 3NF to ensure that every functional dependency in a relation is a dependency on a superkey, eliminating update anomalies.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ### OLAP Concept
>> Later in his career, Codd **coined and defined Online Analytical Processing** — establishing the conceptual distinction between transactional (OLTP) and analytical (OLAP) workloads that continues to shape data architecture today.

<span class="at-kicker">Interesting Facts</span>

## Interesting facts

> [!grid|cols2]
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ### Oracle's Origin
>> Oracle's Larry Ellison was directly inspired by Codd's 1970 paper — he built Oracle to be the first commercial implementation of the relational model. IBM itself was slow to act; Codd's work was initially resisted by IBM management who feared it would cannibalize their IMS hierarchical database product.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ### Forbes Named It One of the Greatest Innovations
>> Forbes (2002) — near the end of Codd's life — recognized the relational model as among the most important innovations in the past 85 years. The **Turing Award** Codd received in 1981 is considered the "Nobel Prize of Computing" — he is one of only a handful of database researchers to receive it.
