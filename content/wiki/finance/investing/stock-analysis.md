# Stock Analysis

<p class="at-lead">
Stock analysis is the systematic process of moving from thousands of listed companies down to a handful of high-conviction investments. It requires screening for quantitative quality, understanding the business qualitatively, verifying with a checklist, and then avoiding the traps that fool even experienced investors.
</p>

<span class="at-stat">4-stage</span> methodology &nbsp;·&nbsp; <span class="at-stat">Screen → Understand → Verify → Value</span> &nbsp;·&nbsp; <span class="at-mark">discipline separating investors from gamblers</span>

<span class="at-kicker">The Methodology</span>

## Stock Selection Methodology

The process follows four major stages:

> [!grid|cols2]
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### STAGE 1
>> ### *Screen* — Financial Ratios
>> Filter stocks using pre-defined quantitative conditions: size, growth, capital efficiency, debt levels, and valuation. This narrows the universe from thousands to a manageable pool.
>> → See [[#ratio-screening|Ratio Screening Criteria]] below
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### STAGE 2
>> ### *Understand* — The Business
>> Read the annual report for at least the last **5 years**. Understand what the company does, who runs it, its competitive position, industry dynamics, and management quality.
>> → See [[#understanding-the-business|Business Understanding Checklist]] below
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### STAGE 3
>> ### *Verify* — Selection Checklist
>> Apply the quantitative selection checklist covering gross margins, revenue growth, EPS consistency, debt levels, inventory, receivables quality, cash flow, and ROE.
>> → See [[#selection-checklist|Selection Checklist]] below
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### STAGE 4
>> ### *Value* — Intrinsic Value
>> Only after Stages 1–3: calculate intrinsic value using DCF analysis. Check for value traps before buying.
>> → [[dcf-valuation|DCF Valuation]] and [[#value-traps|Value Traps]] below

<span class="at-kicker">Ratio Screening</span>

## Stage 1: Ratio Screening Criteria

Stock screening filters for stocks meeting specific quantitative criteria:

| Criteria | Threshold | Ratio/Metric |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Market Cap** | > ₹6,000 Cr (Large & Mid Cap) | — |
| **Revenue CAGR** | > 7% (3 years) | Revenue trend |
| **Profit CAGR** | > 7% (3 years) | PAT trend |
| **Capital Use** | ROCE > 15% | [[../financial-ratios/profitability-ratios\|ROCE]] |
| **Debt Safety** | Debt/Equity < 0.5 | [[../financial-ratios/leverage-ratios\|D/E Ratio]] |
| **Valuation** | Current P/E < 1.5× 5-year avg P/E | [[valuation-ratios\|P/E Ratio]] |
| **Growth Valuation** | PEG < 2 | [[valuation-ratios\|PEG Ratio]] |

> [!tip] Institutional vs. Retail Investor
> | Institutional | Retail Investor |
> |---|---|
> | Multiple analysts at disposal | Personal analysis or paid services |
> | Access to Bloomberg, Reuters, Factset | Based on financial strength & ROI |
> | Access to company management | Limited access |
>
> Retail investors must compensate with discipline, patience, and superior analytical frameworks.

<span class="at-kicker">Understanding the Business</span>

## Stage 2: Understanding the Business

Read the Annual Report thoroughly for the last 5 years. Key questions to answer:

| # | Question | Rationale |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | What does the company do? | Basic understanding of the business |
| 2 | Who are its promoters? What are their backgrounds? | Sanity check — rule out criminal background, intense political affiliation |
| 3 | What do they manufacture/sell? | Understand product demand-supply dynamics |
| 4 | How many plants/locations? | Geographic presence; location value may be off-balance-sheet |
| 5 | Are they running plants at full capacity? | Operational abilities and demand positioning |
| 6 | What raw materials are required? | Dependency risks (government-regulated or imported inputs) |
| 7 | Who are the company's clients? | Sales cycle and effort required to sell |
| 8 | Who are their competitors? | Margin pressure; monopoly/duopoly = higher margins |
| 9 | Who are the major shareholders? | Presence of successful investors = positive signal |
| 10 | Do they plan to launch new products? | Innovation and focus; outside-domain expansion = red flag |
| 11 | Do they plan to expand geographically? | Same rationale as above |
| 12 | What is the revenue mix? | Identifies key growth drivers |
| 13 | Do they operate under heavy regulation? | Natural barrier to entry (good); innovation constraints (bad) |
| 14 | Who are their bankers and auditors? | Rule out associations with scandalous agencies |
| 15 | How many employees? Labour issues? | Labour intensity and niche skill dependency |
| 16 | What are the entry barriers? | How hard for new competitors to enter and erode margins |
| 17 | Can products be replicated in cheap-labour countries? | Long-term competitive risk assessment |
| 18 | Does the company have too many subsidiaries? | Potential fund diversion risk — requires investigation |

<span class="at-kicker">Selection Checklist</span>

## Stage 3: Stock Selection Checklist

After understanding the business qualitatively, verify quantitatively:

| # | Variable | Target | Significance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Gross Profit Margin** | > 20% | Higher margin = stronger sustainable moat |
| 2 | **Revenue Growth** | In line with gross profit growth; aim for 15% CAGR | Revenue growth without profit growth = margin compression |
| 3 | **EPS Consistency** | EPS growth consistent with net profit growth | Divergence = equity dilution occurring |
| 4 | **Debt Level** | Not highly leveraged; use ICR and Debt/EBIT | High debt → finance costs erode earnings |
| 5 | **Inventory** (mfg.) | Growing inventory + growing PAT margin = positive | Rising inventory without margin growth = red flag |
| 6 | **Sales vs. Receivables** | Sales not predominantly backed by receivables | Receivables-backed revenue = company pushing products without payment |
| 7 | **Cash Flow from Operations** | Must be positive | Negative OCF = operating stress regardless of reported profits |
| 8 | **ROE** | > 25% | Higher ROE = better shareholder returns; verify debt levels |
| 9 | **Business Diversity** | 1–2 simple business lines | Multiple interests = difficult to analyse and often underperform |
| 10 | **Subsidiaries** | Not excessive | Too many = potential fund siphoning risk |

<span class="at-kicker">Piotroski Score</span>

## Piotroski Score

The <span class="at-mark">Piotroski Score</span> is a quantitative tool for identifying value stocks, scored from **0 to 9** (9 = strongest fundamentals, 0 = weakest).

### Scoring Framework

> [!grid|cols3]
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### PROFITABILITY (4 pts)
>> ### *Profitability* Criteria
>> - Positive Net Income (1 pt)
>> - Positive Return on Assets this year (1 pt)
>> - Positive Operating Cash Flow this year (1 pt)
>> - OCF > Net Income — earnings quality (1 pt)
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### LEVERAGE & LIQUIDITY (3 pts)
>> ### *Leverage & Liquidity* Criteria
>> - Long-term debt ratio lower than prior year (1 pt)
>> - Current ratio higher than prior year (more liquidity) (1 pt)
>> - No new shares issued in last year — no dilution (1 pt)
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### OPERATING EFFICIENCY (2 pts)
>> ### *Operating Efficiency* Criteria
>> - Gross margin higher than prior year (1 pt)
>> - Asset turnover ratio higher than prior year (1 pt)

| Score Range | Interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| **8–9** | Strong value stock candidate |
| **5–7** | Moderate quality — further research needed |
| **0–4** | Weak fundamentals — avoid or short |

> [!tip] Piotroski in Practice
> The Piotroski Score is best used as a **secondary filter** after initial screening — not as a standalone buy/sell signal. A score of 8–9 in a stock trading at low valuation multiples is a strong combination signal.

<span class="at-kicker">Value Traps</span>

## Value Traps — The Investor's Greatest Enemy

A <span class="at-mark">value trap</span> is a stock that appears cheaply priced on P/E, P/CF, or P/B metrics for an extended period — but continues to languish or fall further despite the apparent cheapness.

> When a stock trades below intrinsic value, it *could* be a value trap — not all cheap stocks are bargains.

### Seven Value Trap Warning Signs

> [!grid|cols2]
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### 1. EARNINGS & CASH FLOW
>> ### Cheap vs. Past, Not Future *Earnings*
>> If a stock's price is very cheap compared to **past** earnings, that's a warning sign. Markets discount **future** cash flows — not past ones. Absurdly cheap vs. past earnings often means something is deeply wrong with the outlook.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### 2. BUSINESS PLAN
>> ### Outdated or *Incomprehensible* Plan
>> Beware of business plans that are unclear, unprofitable, or outdated by technology. If a product/service is obsolete it doesn't matter how cheap the stock is — the business will likely fail.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### 3. MANAGEMENT
>> ### *Management* Quality
>> Poor management can sink almost any company. Red flags: management selling stock, unreliable guidance, dividend cuts. Green flag: management owns and buys company stock (insider buying).
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### 4. ACCOUNTING
>> ### Complex or *Fraudulent* Accounting
>> Complicated or non-transparent financial reports indicate hidden problems. Any hint of fraud eliminates the investment. Quality companies demonstrate openness about both successes and failures.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### 5. BALANCE SHEET / DEBT
>> ### Dangerous *Debt* Levels
>> High debt can cause liquidity and solvency crises even in good businesses. The balance sheet may be more important than the income statement for sorting out value traps. A strong balance sheet provides a margin of safety in adverse conditions.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### 6. STRATEGIC ADVANTAGES
>> ### Missing *Competitive Moat*
>> A company without sustainable competitive advantages cannot protect margins in competitive markets. Does the company have: market leadership, economies of scale, pricing power, product differentiation, cost advantages, or powerful brands?
>> Without at least one, it may not thrive.

> [!warning] The Seventh Trap — Looking Backward
> **The most dangerous trap:** using past earnings to judge cheapness. Most financial sites quote P/E based on **trailing earnings**. Markets value companies on **future earnings and growth**. What a company earned in the past has little to do with its value today.
>
> Looking forward means estimating future earnings and cash flows — then comparing those metrics (not past metrics) to current price. This is why [[dcf-valuation|DCF Valuation]] matters.

<span class="at-kicker">Combining Everything</span>

## The Complete Evaluation Summary

```
SCREEN         → ROCE >15%, D/E <0.5, Revenue/Profit CAGR >7%, PEG <2
UNDERSTAND     → Annual Report (5 yrs), business model, management, moat
VERIFY         → GPM >20%, ROE >25%, positive OCF, EPS consistent with profits
PIOTROSKI      → Score 8-9 as secondary confirmation
VALUE          → DCF: FCF projections, terminal value, intrinsic share price
MARGIN SAFETY  → Buy at 30-50% discount to intrinsic value
VALUE TRAP CHK → Forward earnings? Management quality? Balance sheet strength?
```
