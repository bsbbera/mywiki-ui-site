# Valuation Ratios

<p class="at-lead">
Valuation ratios compare a company's stock price to its profitability, book value, revenue, or overall enterprise value. They are the market's language for expressing how much investors are willing to pay for a unit of business output — and are the first tool for determining whether a stock is cheap or expensive.
</p>

<span class="at-stat">7 key ratios</span> &nbsp;·&nbsp; <span class="at-stat">P/E · PEG · P/B · P/S · EV/EBITDA · Dividend Yield · EPS</span> &nbsp;·&nbsp; <span class="at-mark">the price-to-value translation toolkit</span>

<span class="at-kicker">Earnings-Based Ratios</span>

## Earnings-Based Valuation

### Earnings Per Share (EPS)

$$\text{EPS} = \frac{\text{Net Profit} - \text{Preferred Dividends}}{\text{Number of Common Shares}}$$

Also: **EPS = PAT / Total Number of Shares**

EPS measures **profitability on a per-share basis** — how much the company earns for each unit of ordinary share held.

- Higher EPS → higher per-share profitability
- EPS should be **consistent with net profit trend** — if they diverge, the company may be diluting equity (issuing new shares)
- Dilution reduces EPS even when absolute profits grow — a signal to watch carefully

### Price-to-Earnings Ratio (P/E)

$$\text{P/E} = \frac{\text{Share Price}}{\text{Earnings Per Share}}$$

The P/E ratio is defined as **how much an investor pays for every unit of company earnings**. A P/E of 30 means you pay 30 units of currency for every 1 unit the company earns annually.

> [!info] P/E in Practice
> - P/E is meaningless for **loss-making companies** (negative EPS)
> - **Forward P/E** uses expected future EPS; **Trailing P/E** uses past 12 months EPS
> - P/E ratios are **sector-dependent** — technology companies trade at higher P/Es than utilities
> - Screen: Current P/E < 1.5× the company's 5-year average P/E

### PEG Ratio (Price/Earnings-to-Growth)

$$\text{PEG} = \frac{\text{P/E Ratio}}{\text{EPS Growth Rate}}$$

**Ticker Tape formula:** `PEG = PE Ratio / 1-Year Forward EPS Growth`

The PEG ratio adjusts the P/E for the company's growth rate, making high-P/E growth stocks more comparable to low-P/E value stocks.

| PEG Value | Interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| **PEG < 1** | Potentially undervalued relative to growth |
| **PEG ≈ 1** | Fairly valued — P/E matches growth rate |
| **PEG > 2** | Possibly overvalued — paying too much for growth |
| **PEG > 3** | High premium — growth expectations must be very high |

**Screening target: PEG < 2 (lower is better).**

> [!tip] PEG vs. P/E
> A stock with P/E 30 and 30% earnings growth has PEG = 1 — fairly priced for its growth. A stock with P/E 15 and 5% growth has PEG = 3 — expensive despite the low absolute P/E. PEG reveals the true relative value.

<span class="at-kicker">Asset-Based Ratios</span>

## Asset-Based Valuation

### Price to Book Value (P/B)

**Book Value per Share:**
$$\text{Book Value} = \frac{\text{Share Capital} + \text{Reserves} - \text{Revaluation Reserves}}{\text{Total Number of Shares}}$$

$$\text{P/B Ratio} = \frac{\text{Stock Price}}{\text{Book Value per Share}}$$

Book Value indicates **what shareholders would receive per share if the company were liquidated today** — the net asset value per share.

- **P/B > 1** → Market values the company above its book value (growth/quality premium)
- **P/B < 1** → Market values below book value — potentially undervalued, or assets impaired
- **High P/B** → May indicate overvaluation relative to net assets
- **Low P/B** → May indicate undervaluation — but check for asset quality issues

> [!warning] P/B Limitations
> P/B is most useful for asset-heavy companies (banks, real estate, manufacturing). For asset-light businesses (software, consulting), book value is a poor measure of true value because intangible assets dominate but are not fully captured on the balance sheet.

### Price to Sales (P/S)

$$\text{P/S} = \frac{\text{Current Share Price}}{\text{Sales per Share}}$$

Where: **Sales per Share = Total Revenue / Number of Shares**

For many companies — especially early-stage or loss-making companies — investors use **revenue instead of earnings** to value their investments.

- **Higher P/S** → higher valuation relative to sales
- Useful when earnings are negative or highly volatile
- Compare P/S within the same sector — capital-light businesses with high margins deserve higher P/S

<span class="at-kicker">Enterprise Valuation</span>

## Enterprise Value Ratios

### EV/EBITDA

Enterprise Value (EV) = Market Cap + Total Debt − Cash & Cash Equivalents

$$\text{EV/EBITDA} = \frac{\text{Enterprise Value}}{\text{EBITDA}}$$

EV/EBITDA is particularly powerful for **debt-heavy companies** (e.g., commodity stocks) because it captures the total enterprise value — not just market capitalisation.

> [!info] Why EV/EBITDA for Commodity Stocks
> Debt has a significant impact on earnings during an upcycle because of the **tax shield on interest payments**. As operating cash flows improve in an upcycle:
> - Debt falls
> - EBITDA rises for highly leveraged players
> - The entire debt reduction flows to the equity side
> - This results in significant uplift in market cap
>
> For commodity stocks, **focus is balance-sheet-driven** rather than P&L-driven. EV/EBITDA captures this dynamic better than P/E.

<span class="at-kicker">Income Ratios</span>

## Income-Based Valuation

### Dividend Yield

$$\text{Dividend Yield} = \frac{\text{Annual Dividend per Share}}{\text{Price per Share}}$$

Dividend Yield expresses the annual dividend income as a **percentage of the current stock price**. A stock priced at ₹100 paying ₹4 annual dividend has a 4% yield.

> [!grid|cols2]
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### HIGH YIELD
>> ### *High* Dividend Yield
>> Can indicate undervaluation (price has fallen while dividend held steady) or a high-income business model. Watch for **dividend sustainability** — high yields from declining businesses can be unsustainable (dividend cut risk).
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### LOW YIELD
>> ### *Low* Dividend Yield / No Dividend
>> Growth companies typically pay little or no dividend — they reinvest cash into expansion. Low dividend yield doesn't equal poor return if capital appreciation is strong.

<span class="at-kicker">Ratio Comparison</span>

## Valuation Ratio Quick Reference

| Ratio | Formula | Best For | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **EPS** | (Net Profit − Preferred Div) / Shares | Per-share profitability | Dilution effect |
| **P/E** | Price / EPS | Earnings-based valuation | Meaningless for losses |
| **PEG** | P/E / EPS Growth Rate | Growth-adjusted value | Forward growth estimate uncertainty |
| **P/B** | Price / Book Value per Share | Asset-heavy businesses | Poor for asset-light cos. |
| **P/S** | Price / Sales per Share | Loss-making or high-growth | High P/S = thin margins risky |
| **EV/EBITDA** | EV / EBITDA | Leveraged/capital-intensive cos. | EBITDA ignores CapEx |
| **Dividend Yield** | Annual Div / Price | Income investors | Sustainability check |

<span class="at-kicker">Context and Application</span>

## Using Valuation Ratios in Practice

> [!tip] Always Use Multiple Ratios
> No single valuation ratio is complete. A stock cheap on P/E might be expensive on P/B. A stock with low P/S might have terrible margins. Combine at least 2–3 ratios for a fuller picture.

> [!example] Valuation Screening Workflow
> 1. **Screen by P/E** — is current P/E below historical average and below peers?
> 2. **Check PEG** — is the P/E justified by growth rate? Target PEG < 2
> 3. **Verify with P/B** — is the stock near or below book value?
> 4. **Add EV/EBITDA** — particularly for debt-heavy or cyclical sectors
> 5. **Check Dividend Yield** — income component for total return calculation
