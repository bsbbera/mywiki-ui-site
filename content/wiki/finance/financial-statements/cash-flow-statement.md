# Cash Flow Statement

<p class="at-lead">
The Cash Flow Statement gives a picture of the true cash position — the liquidity — of a company. A business can be profitable on paper yet cash-flow negative in reality. The Cash Flow Statement is the antidote to accounting illusions: it shows exactly where cash came from and where it went.
</p>

<span class="at-stat">3 activities</span> tracked &nbsp;·&nbsp; <span class="at-stat">Operating · Investing · Financing</span> &nbsp;·&nbsp; <span class="at-mark">the true liquidity story behind the profits</span>

<span class="at-kicker">What It Measures</span>

## Purpose and Key Principles

1. The Cash Flow Statement gives a picture of the **true cash position** (liquidity) of the company
2. A legitimate company has three main activities: **operating**, **investing**, and **financing**
3. Each activity either **generates** or **drains** money
4. **Net cash flow = Operating Activities + Investing Activities + Financing Activities**
5. Investors should specifically watch **cash flow from operating activities**
6. The ending cash balance on the Cash Flow Statement ties directly back to **cash & cash equivalents** on the Balance Sheet

<span class="at-kicker">The Three Activities</span>

## Three Major Categories

> [!grid|cols3]
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### OPERATING ACTIVITIES
>> ### *Operating* Activities (OA)
>> Activities related to daily **core business operations**: sales, marketing, manufacturing, technology upgrades, resource hiring. This is the most important section — it shows whether the business generates real cash from its actual operations.
>> > **Key check:** Does operating cash flow align with net income? Large divergence = earnings quality concern.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### INVESTING ACTIVITIES
>> ### *Investing* Activities (IA)
>> Investments made with the intention of reaping benefits **at a later stage**: parking money in interest-bearing instruments, investing in equities, purchasing land, property, plant & equipment, intangibles, and other non-current assets.
>> > Negative investing cash flow is often **healthy** — it means the company is investing in growth.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### FINANCING ACTIVITIES
>> ### *Financing* Activities (FA)
>> All **financial transactions** of the company: distributing dividends, paying interest to service debt, raising fresh debt, issuing corporate bonds, share buybacks, or new equity issuances.
>> > Watch for companies constantly raising new equity — it may signal the business cannot fund itself from operations.

$$\text{Net Cash Flow} = \text{Cash from OA} + \text{Cash from IA} + \text{Cash from FA}$$

<span class="at-kicker">Construction Principles</span>

## Basic Principles of Cash Flow Construction

The cash flow statement is constructed from the balance sheet using two core rules:

> [!info] The Two Fundamental Rules
> **Rule 1 — Liabilities:**
> Whenever a company's **liabilities increase**, the **cash balance increases**. Conversely, when liabilities decrease, cash decreases.
>
> **Rule 2 — Assets:**
> Whenever a company's **assets increase**, the **cash balance decreases**. Conversely, when assets decrease, cash increases.

These rules explain why:
- Taking on new debt (liability ↑) → cash in
- Buying equipment (asset ↑) → cash out
- Collecting receivables (asset ↓) → cash in
- Paying off a loan (liability ↓) → cash out

<span class="at-kicker">Connecting to Balance Sheet</span>

## Balance Sheet Connection

The Cash Flow Statement and Balance Sheet are directly linked:

> [!tip] The Bridge
> The **final value** in the Cash Flow Statement — the net liquid cash — is the same number that appears as **Cash & Cash Equivalents** on the Balance Sheet. This is the fundamental check when reconciling statements.

Additionally:
- Changes in **current assets** on the balance sheet (inventory, receivables) affect operating cash flow
- **CapEx** (investing activities) increases the gross block of PP&E on the balance sheet
- **Debt raised or repaid** (financing activities) changes long-term borrowings on the balance sheet

<span class="at-kicker">Free Cash Flow</span>

## Free Cash Flow (FCF)

<span class="at-mark">Free Cash Flow</span> represents the cash a company generates after accounting for all cash outflows to support operations and maintain capital assets. Unlike net income, FCF excludes non-cash expenses and *includes* actual capital spending.

$$\text{FCF} = \text{Cash from Operating Activities} - \text{Capital Expenditures}$$

FCF is more reliable than net income for valuation because it cannot be manipulated by accounting choices. It is the foundation of DCF (Discounted Cash Flow) valuation.

> [!grid|cols2]
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### WHY FCF MATTERS
>> ### *Free Cash Flow* vs. Net Income
>> Net income includes non-cash items like depreciation and amortisation — it is an **accounting construct**. FCF is a **cash construct** — it accounts for what the business truly generates after maintaining and growing its asset base.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### OWNER'S EARNINGS
>> ### *Owner's Earnings* Method
>> A refinement of FCF: `Owner's Earnings = Operating Cash Flow − Maintenance CapEx`. Maintenance CapEx is the portion of capital spending needed to **maintain current operations** (not grow). Most companies don't report this separately, so analysts estimate it.

<span class="at-kicker">What to Analyse</span>

## Analysing the Cash Flow Statement

### Operating Cash Flow Quality Check

| Scenario | Interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| OCF > Net Income | **Healthy** — the business converts profits to cash efficiently |
| OCF ≈ Net Income | Normal — depreciation and working capital roughly cancel |
| OCF < Net Income | **Warning** — profits may be inflated; working capital may be bloating |
| OCF negative, Net Income positive | **Red flag** — company is consuming cash despite reported profits |

### Capital Expenditure Analysis

> [!tip] CapEx Efficiency
> Compare **CapEx to Operating Cash Flow** over multiple years. A company that can grow while spending less than 50% of earnings on CapEx has a strong competitive advantage — excess cash is available for dividends, buybacks, or further growth investment.

### Cash Flow Checklist for Investors

- [ ] Is operating cash flow **positive and growing**?
- [ ] Does operating cash flow **align with net income**?
- [ ] Is CapEx as a **percentage of earnings** manageable (<50%)?
- [ ] Is the company **not constantly raising equity** to fund operations?
- [ ] Does the ending cash balance **tie back** to the balance sheet?

> [!example] Real-World Insight
> A company showing 20% revenue growth and 15% net income growth is impressive. But if operating cash flow is barely positive or negative, it could mean the growth is funded by extending customer credit (receivables growing) or building inventory — neither of which is cash. Always check OCF alongside the income statement.

<span class="at-kicker">Statement Interplay</span>

## How the Three Statements Connect Through Cash Flow

| Movement | P&L Effect | Balance Sheet Effect | Cash Flow Effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sell goods on credit | Revenue ↑ | Receivables ↑ | No immediate cash |
| Collect receivables | — | Receivables ↓, Cash ↑ | OCF ↑ |
| Buy PP&E | Depreciation expense (future) | PP&E ↑ | IA outflow |
| Take on new debt | Interest expense ↑ | Debt ↑, Cash ↑ | FA inflow |
| Pay dividends | — | Retained earnings ↓ | FA outflow |
| Record depreciation | Expense ↑, Profit ↓ | Accumulated depreciation ↑ | Non-cash add-back in OCF |
