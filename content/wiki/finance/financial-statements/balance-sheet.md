# Balance Sheet

<p class="at-lead">
The Balance Sheet is a financial snapshot — it shows exactly what a company owns, what it owes, and what's left for shareholders at a <em>specific point in time</em>. It is the foundation of solvency analysis and the one statement that accumulates a company's entire financial history from incorporation.
</p>

<span class="at-stat">snapshot</span> at a specific date &nbsp;·&nbsp; <span class="at-stat">Assets = Liabilities + Equity</span> &nbsp;·&nbsp; <span class="at-mark">the definitive measure of financial health</span>

<span class="at-kicker">The Accounting Equation</span>

## The Fundamental Equation

$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Shareholders' Equity}$$

Or equivalently:

$$\text{Shareholders' Equity} = \text{Assets} - \text{Liabilities}$$

The balance sheet has three sections:
1. **Assets** — resources with economic value owned by the company
2. **Liabilities** — obligations the company must settle
3. **Shareholders' Equity** — the residual interest after liabilities are deducted from assets

> [!info] Flow Basis
> Unlike the P&L and Cash Flow Statement (which cover a specific year), the Balance Sheet is prepared on a **flow basis** — it accumulates financial information from the day the company was incorporated. Balance sheet numbers are set for a **specific date**, making it a snapshot rather than a period report.

<span class="at-kicker">Assets</span>

## Assets

An <span class="at-mark">asset</span> is a resource with economic value that the company owns or controls, expected to provide economic value in the future. Assets are reported in order of **liquidity** (most liquid first).

**Total Assets = Current Assets + Non-Current Assets**

### Current Assets

Current assets can be **easily converted to cash** and are expected to be consumed within **365 days**. They fund day-to-day operations.

> [!grid|cols3]
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### CASH & EQUIVALENTS
>> ### *Cash* & Short-Term Investments
>> The most liquid component. Includes short-term CDs, 3-month treasuries, and other liquid assets. Required for day-to-day operations.
>> > Cash is king in troubled times. A business with lots of cash and little debt can sail through adversity when competitors cannot.
>> **Three ways to create cash:** sell bonds/equity, sell existing business, generate more from ongoing operations.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### INVENTORY
>> ### *Total* Inventory
>> Products warehoused and ready to sell. **Critical variable:** take Total Inventory / Net Earnings — if both rise together, the company has a profitable way to grow sales. If inventory grows without corresponding earnings, the company may be in a boom-bust cycle.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### NET RECEIVABLES
>> ### *Net* Receivables
>> Money owed to the firm by customers for goods/services delivered but not yet paid. Net Receivables = Gross Receivables minus estimated bad debts allowance.
>> > Companies with consistently **lower Net Receivables to Gross Sales** ratios than competitors have a competitive advantage.

Other current asset components:
- **Short-term Investments** — marketable securities due within a year
- **Prepaid Expenses** — goods/services paid for but not yet received

### Non-Current Assets (Fixed Assets)

Non-current assets have economic benefits enjoyed **beyond 365 days**. They cannot be easily liquidated.

**Net Block = Gross Block − Accumulated Depreciation**

> [!grid|cols3]
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### PROPERTY/PLANT/EQUIPMENT
>> ### *PP&E* — Tangible Fixed Assets
>> Physical assets: manufacturing plant, equipment, buildings, land, vehicles. Carried at **original cost less accumulated depreciation**.
>> - **Competitive Advantage:** Company doesn't need frequent upgrades
>> - **Competitive Disadvantage:** Must constantly upgrade to keep pace with competition
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### GOODWILL
>> ### *Goodwill* — Acquisition Premium
>> When a company acquires another at a price exceeding market valuation, the excess is recorded as Goodwill.
>> `Goodwill = Purchase Price − (Fair Market Value of Assets − Fair Market Value of Liabilities)`
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### INTANGIBLE ASSETS
>> ### *Intangible* Assets
>> Economic value without physical form: patents, copyrights, trademarks, designs, franchise agreements. Benefits companies over long periods and is therefore classified as a fixed asset.

Additional non-current assets:
- **Long-Term Investments** — stocks, bonds, real estate with >1 year horizon
- **Other Long-Term Assets** — prepaid expenses, future tax recoveries, anything with >1 year useful life

<span class="at-kicker">Liabilities</span>

## Liabilities

A <span class="at-mark">liability</span> represents a company obligation. Companies take on obligations because they believe these obligations will provide economic value in the long run.

**Total Liabilities = Current Liabilities + Non-Current Liabilities**

### Current Liabilities

Current liabilities are obligations expected to be settled within **365 days**. The term 'Current' signals near-term settlement.

> [!grid|cols2]
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### SHORT-TERM BORROWINGS
>> ### *Short-Term* Debt
>> Short-term loans for working capital requirements. Historically cheaper than long-term money.
>> > **Key ratio:** Short-term Debt / Long-term Debt — reveals the leverage structure. Companies that borrow short and lend long take on refinancing risk.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### ACCOUNTS PAYABLE
>> ### *Trade* Payables & Accrued Expenses
>> **Accounts Payable:** money owed to suppliers for goods/services received on credit. **Accrued Expenses:** sales tax payable, wages payable, accrued rent. **Other Current Liabilities:** catch-all for short-term obligations not fitting above categories.

### Non-Current Liabilities

Long-term obligations settled **after 12 months** from the reporting date.

| Component | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Long-Term Borrowings** | Most important line item — key input for leverage ratios. Rule of thumb: company should be able to repay from earnings within **3–4 years** |
| **Deferred Tax Liability** | Provision for future tax payments the company anticipates paying |
| **Long-Term Provisions** | Employee benefits: gratuity, leave encashment, provident funds |

<span class="at-kicker">Shareholders' Equity</span>

## Shareholders' Equity

Shareholders' equity — also called **book value** — is what remains after subtracting all liabilities from all assets. It represents the amount shareholders have invested and left to keep the business running.

**Equity = Assets − Liabilities**

> [!grid|cols2]
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### COMMON & PREFERRED STOCK
>> ### *Equity* Capital
>> **Common Stock:** represents ownership and voting rights. Common stockholders elect the board of directors, receive dividends (if declared), and share proceeds if the company is sold. **Preferred Stock:** no voting rights but receive fixed/adjustable dividends before common shareholders, and have priority in bankruptcy.
>> **Additional Paid-in Capital:** any amount paid above par value when stock was issued.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### RETAINED EARNINGS
>> ### *Retained* Earnings
>> Accumulated profits not distributed as dividends. Each year, net income is added (or losses subtracted) from the running total of retained earnings since incorporation.
>> > **Critical line item for competitive advantage analysis.** Consistently growing retained earnings indicates a business that compounds value over time.

### Treasury Stock

Treasury stock is outstanding stock **bought back** from shareholders by the company. These shares are issued but no longer outstanding — they are excluded from dividend distributions and EPS calculations. Share buybacks reduce the total share count, boosting EPS for remaining shareholders.

### Reserve and Surplus

- **Reserves** — money earmarked for specific corporate purposes
- **Surplus** — where all the company's accumulated profits reside

<span class="at-kicker">Interpreting the Balance Sheet</span>

## Key Analytical Signals

| Signal | What to Check | Implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cash vs. Debt** | High cash + low debt | Strong financial resilience |
| **Current Ratio** | Current Assets / Current Liabilities > 1 | Adequate short-term liquidity |
| **Retained Earnings Trend** | Growing year-on-year | Compounding business quality |
| **Long-Term Debt** | Repayable within 3–4 years of earnings | Manageable leverage |
| **Goodwill** | As % of total assets | High goodwill = acquisition risk |
| **Net Block Trend** | Stable without constant upgrade | Potential competitive advantage |
| **Receivables / Sales** | Low vs. competitors | Revenue is cash-backed |

> [!tip] Accounts Receivable Watch
> Sales growth backed by receivables growth (without cash conversion) is a **red flag**. It can mean the company is pushing products to record revenue without customers actually paying — a sign of potential earnings quality issues.
