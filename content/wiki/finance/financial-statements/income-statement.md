# Income Statement

<p class="at-lead">
The Income Statement — also called the P&L (Profit & Loss), Statement of Operations, or Statement of Earnings — reports every dollar the company earned and spent over a specific period. It is the definitive answer to one question: <em>Is this business profitable?</em>
</p>

<span class="at-stat">top-line</span> revenue to <span class="at-stat">bottom-line</span> net income &nbsp;·&nbsp; <span class="at-mark">the profitability story of a business</span>

<span class="at-kicker">Statement Structure</span>

## The Income Statement Format

The general format flows from revenue at the top, deducting costs layer by layer to arrive at net income:

| Line Item | Type | Example ($M) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Revenue** | Inflow | 10,000 |
| **Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)** | Outflow | 3,000 |
| **Gross Profit** | Revenue − COGS | 7,000 |
| **Operating Expenses** | | |
| &nbsp;&nbsp; SG&A (Selling, General & Admin) | Outflow | 2,100 |
| &nbsp;&nbsp; R&D Costs | Outflow | 1,000 |
| &nbsp;&nbsp; Interest Expense | Outflow | 700 |
| &nbsp;&nbsp; Gain/(Loss) on Asset Sales | Inflow/Outflow | 1,490 |
| **Profit Before Tax (PBT)** | — | 1,500 |
| **Income Tax** | Outflow | 525 |
| **Profit After Tax (PAT) / Net Income** | — | 975 |

<span class="at-kicker">Revenue</span>

## Revenue — The Top Line

Revenue is the **top line** of the income statement. It indicates the total amount of money that flows into the company during the fiscal period.

> [!info] Revenue vs. Earnings
> Earnings can be calculated by subtracting total expenses from Revenue. A good company improves earnings either by **increasing revenue over time** or **reducing expenses** while keeping revenue constant — or ideally both.

<span class="at-kicker">Cost of Goods Sold</span>

## Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)

<span class="at-mark">Cost of Goods Sold</span> — also called **Cost of Revenue** — is the cost of purchasing the goods the company resells, or the cost of materials and labour used in production.

- For product companies: raw materials + direct labour + manufacturing overhead
- For service companies: the term **Cost of Revenue** is used, since no physical goods are produced

<span class="at-kicker">Gross Profit</span>

## Gross Profit

**Gross Profit = Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold**

Gross Profit represents income remaining after **production costs** only. It does *not* include:
- Sales & administrative costs
- Depreciation
- Interest costs

### Gross Profit Margin

**Gross Profit Margin = Gross Profit / Total Revenue**

> [!tip] Competitive Advantage Signal
> Companies with high and **consistent** Gross Profit Margins tend to have a durable competitive advantage — a moat. However, high gross margins can still produce poor overall performance if R&D, SG&A, or interest costs are excessive.

<span class="at-kicker">Operating Expenses</span>

## Operating Expenses

<span class="at-mark">Operating expenses</span> are unavoidable costs required for the smooth day-to-day running of the business. The three main categories:

### SG&A — Selling, General & Administrative Expenses

SG&A is the umbrella term for all costs **not directly tied to making a product**:

> [!grid|cols3]
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### SELLING EXPENSES
>> ### *Direct* Selling
>> Costs incurred when the product is sold: shipping, delivery charges, sales commissions. These vary with sales volume.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### INDIRECT SELLING
>> ### *Indirect* Selling
>> Advertising, marketing, telephone, travel, salaries of sales personnel. Occur throughout the manufacturing process and after.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### G&A EXPENSES
>> ### *General &* Admin
>> Fixed in nature: rent/mortgage, utilities, insurance, salaries of non-sales/production departments.

**SG&A / Gross Profit** — this ratio illuminates how much is being spent on general expenses:
- **Fluctuating** SG&A/GP ratio → indicates poor business management
- **High** SG&A/GP ratio → company unable to contain expenses while revenue may be dropping
- **Low** SG&A/GP → could indicate higher R&D expenditure instead

### R&D Costs

R&D expenses are direct expenditures relating to a company's efforts to develop, design, and enhance products, services, technologies, or processes.

> [!warning] R&D Risk
> Companies with **high R&D costs** put their long-term economics at risk. New products from R&D require updated sales programs, leading to higher SG&A expenses, which further erode gross profits. Always compare R&D costs as a percentage of gross profit across periods.

### Interest Expense

Interest expense is the cost incurred for borrowed funds — it represents interest **accrued** during the period, not necessarily interest paid.

$$\frac{\text{Interest Expense}}{\text{Operating Income}}$$

The ratio of interest payments to operating income reveals the level of risk taken on by the company. High ratios indicate significant debt burden relative to operations.

### Gain (or Loss) on Sale of Assets

When a company sells an asset (other than inventory), the profit or loss is recorded here. The gain/loss is the **difference between proceeds and the carrying amount** on the books. Also includes licensing agreements and sales of patents categorised outside normal business operations.

<span class="at-kicker">Profit Metrics</span>

## Profit Metrics Cascade

> [!grid|cols3]
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### EBITDA
>> ### *EBITDA* Margin
>> **Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation & Amortization.** Measures management efficiency at the pure operating level — before financing costs and accounting adjustments.
>> `EBITDA = Operating Revenue − Operating Expense`
>> `EBITDA Margin = EBITDA / (Total Revenue − Other Income)`
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### OPERATING INCOME
>> ### *Operating* Income
>> **Operating Income = Gross Profit − Operating Expenses.** Also known as EBIT (when depreciation is excluded from operating expenses). Shows profitability from core business operations.
>> `EBIT = EBITDA − Depreciation & Amortization`
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### NET INCOME
>> ### *Net* Income (PAT)
>> **PAT = PBT − Applicable Taxes.** The bottom line — profit after all expenses including interest and taxes. Also called Net Income After Taxes (NIAT).
>> `PAT Margin = PAT / Total Revenue`

### Profit Before Tax (PBT)

PBT — also called **Earnings Before Tax (EBT)** or **Pre-tax Profit** — is Operating Income after deducting all operating expenses, **before** deducting taxes.

**PBT = Total Revenue − Total Operating Expenses**

### Income Tax

The income tax paid on total profit. 

> [!warning] Fraud Detection
> If a company shows high profits but pays unusually low income taxes, compare the stated tax against what should be paid on the PBT. Significant differences can indicate **overstated profits** — a well-known accounting manipulation technique.

<span class="at-kicker">Non-Cash Charges</span>

## Amortization & Depreciation

Depreciation and amortization are non-cash charges that reduce asset values on the balance sheet and reduce taxable income.

> [!grid|cols2]
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### DEPRECIATION
>> ### *Depreciation* — Tangible Assets
>> The expensing of a **fixed (tangible) asset** over its useful life. Applies to: buildings, equipment, office furniture, vehicles, machinery, land. Represents how much of an asset's value has been consumed.
>> > Depreciation eats into company profits — watch the **Depreciation/Gross Margin** ratio. Higher values indicate higher debt likelihood and lower earnings.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### AMORTIZATION
>> ### *Amortization* — Intangible Assets
>> The spreading of an **intangible asset's cost** over its useful life. Applies to: patents, trademarks, franchise agreements, proprietary processes (copyrights), cost of issuing bonds, organisational costs.

### Capital Expenditure (CapEx)

CapEx measures how much a company is investing in existing and new fixed assets to maintain or grow the business.

> [!tip] CapEx Rule of Thumb
> If a company is using **50% or less** of its annual earnings for CapEx, the company is worth considering as an investment — it retains significant earnings for other purposes like dividends, buybacks, or growth.

<span class="at-kicker">Putting It Together</span>

## Reading the Income Statement — Key Signals

| Signal | What to Look For | Implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Revenue Growth** | 15%+ CAGR | Strong business momentum |
| **Gross Margin Stability** | Consistent or improving | Durable competitive advantage |
| **SG&A/Gross Profit** | Low and stable | Efficient cost management |
| **R&D/Gross Profit** | Watch trend | High = innovation risk + margin pressure |
| **PAT Margin** | High relative to peers | Competitive strength |
| **Tax vs. PBT** | Should align at stated rate | Divergence = potential fraud signal |
| **EPS trend** | Growing with net profit | No equity dilution |
