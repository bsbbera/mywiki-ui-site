# Profitability Ratios

<p class="at-lead">
Profitability ratios help analysts measure how efficiently a company generates profit relative to its revenue, assets, and equity. They convey management competitiveness and signal whether a business has a durable economic advantage — because profits are what fund expansion and reward shareholders.
</p>

<span class="at-stat">5 key ratios</span> &nbsp;·&nbsp; <span class="at-stat">ROE · ROA · ROCE · Gross Margin · EBITDA Margin</span> &nbsp;·&nbsp; <span class="at-mark">the scorecard of management performance</span>

<span class="at-kicker">Margin Ratios</span>

## Margin Ratios — Revenue-Based

Margin ratios measure how much of each dollar of revenue becomes profit at various stages of the income statement.

### Gross Profit Margin

$$\text{Gross Profit Margin} = \frac{\text{Gross Profit}}{\text{Total Revenue}}$$

Where: **Gross Profit = Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold**

> [!tip] Moat Signal
> Companies with **high and consistent Gross Profit Margins** tend to have a competitive durable advantage. However, high gross margins can still result in poor overall performance if R&D, SG&A, or interest costs are excessive.

### EBITDA Margin

$$\text{EBITDA Margin} = \frac{\text{EBITDA}}{\text{Total Revenue} - \text{Other Income}}$$

Where:
- `EBITDA = Operating Revenue − Operating Expense`
- `Operating Revenue = Total Revenue − Other Income`
- `Operating Expense = Total Expense − Finance Cost − Depreciation & Amortization`
- `EBIT = EBITDA − Depreciation & Amortization`

<span class="at-mark">EBITDA Margin</span> indicates the efficiency of management — how profitable the company is at the **operating level**, before financing costs and accounting adjustments.

### Net Profit Margin (PAT Margin)

$$\text{PAT Margin} = \frac{\text{PAT}}{\text{Total Revenue}}$$

Where: **PAT = PBT − Applicable Taxes**

- Companies with **high PAT Margins** are more competitive
- PAT Margin is the ultimate "bottom-line" efficiency measure
- PAT growth CAGR is one of the first screeners to check

> [!grid|cols3]
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### GROSS MARGIN
>> ### *Gross* Margin
>> Measures profitability after production costs only. Excludes admin, interest, and taxes. Best for assessing product/service economics and competitive positioning.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### EBITDA MARGIN
>> ### *EBITDA* Margin
>> Operating profitability before interest, taxes, depreciation, amortisation. Useful for comparing companies with different capital structures or depreciation policies.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### NET MARGIN
>> ### *Net* Margin (PAT)
>> True bottom-line profitability after all costs. The ultimate measure of overall profitability — but can be distorted by leverage (interest) and tax rates.

<span class="at-kicker">Return Ratios</span>

## Return Ratios — Capital-Based

Return ratios measure how effectively the company generates profits from the capital invested in it.

### Return on Equity (ROE)

$$\text{ROE} = \frac{\text{Net Profit}}{\text{Shareholders' Equity}} \times 100$$

ROE is a **critical ratio** — it measures the return shareholders earn for every unit of capital invested. Higher ROE = better efficiency in generating profits for shareholders.

**Target:** ROE > 25% is a strong signal for investors.

#### The DuPont Decomposition

$$\text{ROE} = \underbrace{\frac{\text{Net Profit}}{\text{Net Sales}}}_{\text{Profit Margin}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Net Sales}}{\text{Avg Total Assets}}}_{\text{Asset Turnover}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Avg Total Assets}}{\text{Shareholders' Equity}}}_{\text{Financial Leverage}}$$

> [!warning] ROE Flaw
> ROE can be **artificially inflated by high debt**. A company can boost ROE by borrowing more (increasing financial leverage) without any improvement in underlying operations. The DuPont model decomposes ROE to reveal *why* it's high — is it from better margins, better asset use, or just more debt?

### Return on Assets (ROA)

$$\text{ROA} = \frac{\text{Net Income} + \text{Interest} \times (1 - \text{Tax Rate})}{\text{Average Total Assets}}$$

ROA indicates **management's ability to limit investments in non-productive assets**. Higher ROA = better asset productivity.

The interest adjustment removes the effect of the financing choice (debt vs. equity) so the ratio purely reflects the return on deployed assets regardless of how they're funded.

> [!info] ROA vs. ROE
> ROA excludes the effect of leverage; ROE includes it. A company with high ROE but low ROA is likely using significant debt to juice shareholder returns — which is riskier.

### Return on Capital Employed (ROCE)

$$\text{ROCE} = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Capital Employed}}$$

Where: **Capital Employed = Total Equity + Total Debt (both short and long term)**

ROCE indicates overall **profitability taking into account all capital** — both equity and debt. It is the most comprehensive profitability ratio for companies that use both equity and debt financing.

**Target:** ROCE > 15% for quality stock screening.

> [!grid|cols3]
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### ROE
>> ### *Return on* Equity
>> **Net Profit / Equity × 100**
>> Best for: measuring shareholder returns. Watch debt levels alongside — high debt can inflate ROE artificially. Use DuPont to decompose.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### ROA
>> ### *Return on* Assets
>> **(Net Income + Interest×(1−Tax)) / Avg Assets**
>> Best for: asset-heavy industries. Strips out leverage effect — shows pure operational efficiency of the asset base.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### ROCE
>> ### *Return on Capital* Employed
>> **EBIT / Capital Employed**
>> Best for: companies using both debt and equity. The most complete profitability picture — includes all capital in the denominator.

<span class="at-kicker">Comparison Framework</span>

## Benchmarks and Interpretation

| Ratio | Strong | Adequate | Weak |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Gross Margin** | >40% | 20–40% | <20% |
| **EBITDA Margin** | >25% | 15–25% | <15% |
| **PAT Margin** | >15% | 8–15% | <8% |
| **ROE** | >25% | 15–25% | <15% |
| **ROA** | >10% | 5–10% | <5% |
| **ROCE** | >20% | 15–20% | <15% |

*Note: benchmarks are general guidelines; always compare within the same sector.*

> [!example] Reading Profitability Together
> A company with 45% gross margin but 5% net margin likely has excessive SG&A, R&D, or interest costs eating into the gross. Investigate each layer: Gross → EBITDA → Operating → PBT → PAT. Where the biggest drop occurs is where the problem lies.
