# Leverage Ratios

<p class="at-lead">
Leverage ratios — also called solvency or gearing ratios — measure a company's ability to sustain operations over the long term by revealing how much debt it uses to finance growth. They answer one essential question: <em>Can this company survive its obligations?</em>
</p>

<span class="at-stat">long-term</span> sustainability &nbsp;·&nbsp; <span class="at-stat">Debt/Equity · Debt/Assets · Interest Coverage · Financial Leverage</span> &nbsp;·&nbsp; <span class="at-mark">the solvency lens on a business</span>

<span class="at-kicker">Why Leverage Matters</span>

## Why Leverage Ratios Matter

For a company to sustain operations, it must pay its bills and obligations. Leverage ratios reveal:
- **How much debt** the company carries relative to its equity and assets
- **Whether earnings** are sufficient to service interest payments
- **The risk level** investors take on when owning the stock or extending credit

> [!warning] Context is Critical
> Leverage ratios must always be evaluated in context:
> - Compare to **historical values** — is leverage increasing or decreasing?
> - Compare to **industry norms** — capital-intensive industries (utilities, real estate) carry more debt by nature
> - Compare to **earnings power** — can the company repay its debt within 3–4 years?

<span class="at-kicker">Debt Structure Ratios</span>

## Debt Structure Ratios

### Debt to Equity Ratio (D/E)

$$\text{Debt/Equity} = \frac{\text{Total Debt}}{\text{Total Equity}}$$

Where: **Total Debt = Long-Term Borrowings + Short-Term Borrowings**

The D/E ratio reveals how the company is financing its operations — through shareholder funds or creditor funds. A D/E ratio of 1 means equal debt and equity; above 1 means more debt than equity.

> [!info] Source
> Total Debt and Total Equity are both found on the Balance Sheet.

**Target for stock screening:** D/E < 0.5 is a conservative threshold indicating low financial risk.

### Debt to Asset Ratio

$$\text{Debt/Assets} = \frac{\text{Total Debt}}{\text{Total Assets}}$$

This ratio helps understand the **asset financing pattern** — how much of the total asset base is financed through debt capital.

> [!warning] Higher = Riskier
> The higher the Debt to Asset ratio, the more leveraged the company — and therefore the riskier it is. In economic downturns, highly leveraged companies face far greater stress.

### Financial Leverage Ratio

$$\text{Financial Leverage} = \frac{\text{Average Total Assets}}{\text{Average Total Equity}}$$

Financial leverage gives an indication of **the extent to which assets are supported by equity**. Higher values mean more assets are funded by debt relative to equity.

> [!grid|cols3]
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### DEBT/EQUITY
>> ### *Debt to* Equity
>> **Total Debt / Total Equity**
>> Most widely used leverage ratio. Directly shows the balance between creditor and owner funding. Key input in DuPont ROE decomposition — high D/E can inflate ROE.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### DEBT/ASSETS
>> ### *Debt to* Assets
>> **Total Debt / Total Assets**
>> Shows what fraction of assets creditors own (vs. shareholders). Higher = more leveraged asset base. Useful for asset-heavy companies.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### FINANCIAL LEVERAGE
>> ### *Financial* Leverage Ratio
>> **Avg Total Assets / Avg Total Equity**
>> The third component of the DuPont ROE decomposition. Captures how equity is being amplified by total assets including all liabilities.

<span class="at-kicker">Debt Service Ratios</span>

## Debt Service Ratios

### Interest Coverage Ratio

$$\text{Interest Coverage Ratio} = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Interest Payment}}$$

Also called **Debt Service Coverage Ratio** or **Debt Service Ratio**. This ratio measures **how much the company earns relative to its interest burden**.

Where:
- `EBIT = EBITDA − Depreciation & Amortization`
- `Interest Payment` = interest expense from the income statement

**Higher Interest Coverage Ratio = better ability to service debt.**

| ICR Range | Interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| **> 3×** | Comfortable — company easily services its debt |
| **1.5 – 3×** | Adequate — manageable but watch for deterioration |
| **< 1.5×** | Danger zone — earnings may not cover interest reliably |
| **< 1×** | Critical — the company cannot cover its interest expense |

### Current Ratio (Liquidity)

$$\text{Current Ratio} = \frac{\text{Current Assets}}{\text{Current Liabilities}}$$

Also called the **Working Capital Ratio**. This measures a company's ability to pay **short-term obligations** within one year.

> [!info] Current Ratio Nuances
> - Generally preferred: **Current Ratio > 1**
> - Some well-performing companies have Current Ratio < 1 — this can be explained by generous dividends or stock repurchases depleting current assets
> - Strong companies can always tap **commercial paper** to solve short-term cash crunches
> - A ratio well above 1 can also indicate poor working capital management (too much idle inventory or cash)

<span class="at-kicker">Long-Term Debt Guidance</span>

## Analysing Long-Term Debt

Long-term borrowings are one of the **most important items on the balance sheet**. Key questions:

> [!tip] The 3–4 Year Rule
> A useful rule of thumb: **if a company can repay its total long-term debt within 3–4 years from annual earnings, the debt level is manageable**. Compare: Long-Term Debt / Net Income (or EBITDA).

Short-term vs. long-term debt ratio analysis:
- Short-term debt is historically cheaper than long-term debt
- Companies that **borrow short to lend long** take on refinancing risk
- Check: **Short-Term Debt / Long-Term Debt** to understand leverage structure

<span class="at-kicker">Leverage in Context</span>

## Leverage and the DuPont Framework

Leverage ratios don't operate in isolation — they directly affect ROE through the DuPont model:

$$\text{ROE} = \text{Net Margin} \times \text{Asset Turnover} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Total Assets}}{\text{Equity}}}_{\text{Financial Leverage}}$$

A company with high ROE but **high D/E and low ROA** is generating shareholder returns primarily through leverage — a fragile state that works in good times but is dangerous in downturns.

> [!example] Leverage Trap
> Company A: ROE = 30%, D/E = 0.2, ROA = 25% → **Quality** — returns driven by real profitability
> Company B: ROE = 30%, D/E = 3.0, ROA = 8% → **Caution** — returns driven by financial leverage; exposed in a downturn
