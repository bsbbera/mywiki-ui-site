# Comparative Advantage

<p class="at-lead">
Comparative Advantage is an economic principle stating that agents (individuals, firms, or countries) can gain from trade by specialising in producing goods for which they have the lowest opportunity cost, even if they are less efficient in absolute terms.
</p>

## Overview

David Ricardo formalised the concept in 1817 using the example of England and Portugal trading cloth and wine. The insight is powerful: mutual gains from trade exist whenever relative production costs differ, making specialisation and exchange superior to autarky for all parties.

The principle extends to modern trade theory, labour economics, and corporate strategy. However, real-world complications include distributional effects (some groups lose from trade), transport costs, economies of scale, and strategic trade policy. The Leontief paradox questioned whether comparative advantage holds empirically for factor endowments.
