# Security Analysis

<p class="at-lead">
Published in 1934 at the depth of the Great Depression, <em>Security Analysis</em> laid the intellectual and methodological foundation for the discipline of fundamental investing. It introduced rigorous financial statement analysis as the basis for security valuation — a radical departure from the pure speculation that dominated pre-crash Wall Street.
</p>

<span class="at-stat">1934</span> first edition &nbsp;·&nbsp; <span class="at-stat">6 editions</span> over 90 years &nbsp;·&nbsp; <span class="at-mark">the founding text of fundamental analysis</span>

## Core Contributions

> [!grid|cols2]
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### FOUNDATIONAL DEFINITION
>> ### Investment vs. Speculation
>> Graham and Dodd drew a bright line between an **investment operation** (supported by thorough analysis, promising safety of principal and adequate return) and **speculation** (everything else). This distinction — still contested — became the bedrock of value investing philosophy.
>
>> [!card|section]
>> ###### BALANCE SHEET FOCUS
>> ### Earnings Power Value
>> The book introduced systematic methods for analysing a company's **earnings power**, **asset value**, and **financial strength** using balance sheets and income statements — concepts that were not standard practice before 1934.

## Key Takeaways

- **Net-net investing**: Graham's original strategy — buy companies trading below their net current asset value (current assets minus all liabilities). These "cigar-butt" stocks offer a margin of safety even if the business is mediocre.
- **Earnings power value (EPV)**: a company's value based on its normalized earnings capacity, independent of growth assumptions.
- **Financial statement red flags**: the book catalogued warning signs in accounting statements that often precede corporate failure — still relevant.
- **Bond analysis**: half the book covers fixed-income securities, which were the dominant investment vehicle of the 1930s.
